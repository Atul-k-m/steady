# Barriers to healthcare access and medication adherence among older adults (60+) in India: annotated bibliography

Compiled 2026-11-15. Scope: empirical studies only, India only, older adults. Digital health, telemedicine, ABHA/ABDM and eSanjeevani are excluded because another researcher covers them.

How to read this file:
- Numbers are copied exactly as printed in the source. Where a paper contradicts itself, both printed values are given and the conflict is flagged.
- "Opened" says how much of each paper was read: full text, or abstract only.
- Stage tags: S1 Find doctor · S2 Book · S3 Register · S4 Wait/navigate · S5 Consultation · S6 Tests · S7 Reports · S8 Medicines · S9 Follow-ups · S10 Payments/insurance · S11 Digital/teleconsult · S12 Emergencies · S0 Other (distance, transport, general access).
- The ages a study covers are written as "Age band". Two studies do not use 60+: B3 uses 45+ and B5 uses 50+. B10 uses 65+.

## Which barriers do studies most consistently report, and how large are they?

### Takeaway
The barriers that recur most often are distance and transport (S0/S1), cost and out-of-pocket spending (S10), and, for medicines, forgetting, cost and unreliable supply (S8). The largest figures are financial. In NSS 2017–18, the catastrophic health expenditure headcount at the 10% threshold was 46.51% (B13; the table's "Total obs." is 22,452, of 42,762 older adults in the sample). In a rural West Bengal clinic, 72.3% of poor adherers named irregular government pharmacy supply and 66.3% named non-affordability (B8). Non-adherence to chronic-disease medicines was 38.6% in a Karnataka tertiary OPD (B7) and 55.6% in rural Udupi (B6). B8 reported 44.63% adherent. Older adults rarely rate waiting time and communication as "bad" in national survey data: at most 4.6% of outpatients did so (B3). Qualitative studies, however, describe waits of 2 to 4 hours (B11). The two kinds of measure disagree.

**B1. Misra S, Yadav J, Sinha A, Sahoo KC, Tanwar S, Shalini S, Chauhan A, Pati S. (2025). "Miles to go before I seek": distance to the health facility and health care use among older adults in India. *The Lancet Regional Health – Southeast Asia*, 37:100579.**
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12230409/
- Opened: full text, via Europe PMC.
- Article type: published as a **"Comment"**, a short format, although it reports original analysis of LASI data. A corrigendum was issued in *Lancet Reg Health SE Asia* 2025;45:100702. What the corrigendum changed was NOT FOUND.
- Sample size: 31,902 older adults. Age band: 60+.
- Setting: national, all states, rural and urban. Community household survey (LASI Wave 1, 2017–18), secondary cross-sectional analysis.
- Key findings, as printed:
  - "Older adults, on an average travelled a distance of nine miles (14.54 km) to seek outpatient services and 27 miles (43.62 km) for inpatient care."
  - "for two thirds (67%) of the urban older adults, the availed outpatient facility was within six miles (10 km) of reach and for rural counterparts the same was 17.5 miles (28.3 km)."
  - For inpatient care, "the distance and time taken was two times higher for rural sexagenarian than their urban counterparts."
  - "For in-patient admission, 95 percent arranged their own mode of transport, while five percent used ambulance services, with no significant urban[-rural difference]."
  - "Both out-patient and in-patient care utilization was high (73% and 40% respectively) when the facility distance was within 10 km… 17% and 10% for facilities at a distance of 11–30 km and 30 km or more respectively."
  - "for women, those living alone, and with low education and income, this decline was more pronounced." The text gives no numbers for this.
  - "Around 19% of rural older adults had to travel at least 60 km to avail in-patient care… urban dwellers with 10%."
  - Table 1, share whose facility was within 0–10 km, as printed: inpatient rural 28.3, urban 67.7; outpatient rural 67.1, urban 87.2.
- Main limitation:
  - The paper has no limitations section, which is typical of the Comment format.
  - The distances cover only people who used care, so older adults who forwent care are not captured.
  - The design is cross-sectional.
  - **The text and Table 1 appear to disagree.** The text gives "67%" for urban outpatient within 10 km; Table 1 gives 87.2 for urban and 67.1 for rural. The "28.3" that the text gives as a rural distance matches the rural inpatient 0–10 km cell of Table 1.
- Quality: peer-reviewed journal (Comment), analysing a government survey (LASI).
- Stages: S0, S1, S12.

**B2. Das J, Kundu S, Hossain B. (2023). Rural-urban difference in meeting the need for healthcare and food among older adults: evidence from India. *BMC Public Health*, 23:1231.**
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-16126-4 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10294478/
- Opened: full text.
- Sample size: 31,464. Age band: 60+.
- Setting: national, rural and urban. Community survey (LASI Wave 1, 2017–18), cross-sectional with sampling weights. Methods: logistic regression and decomposition analysis.
- Definition of unmet healthcare need: the respondent reported "poor and very poor perceived health" and "did not visit any healthcare facility in the past 365 days."
- Key findings, as printed:
  - "the proportion of older adults having poor perceived health and not visiting any health facility was 8% in rural areas and 3% in urban are[as]."
  - Rural vs urban, unadjusted: OR = 1.17 (CI 1.04–1.33). Adjusted: 1.05 (0.91–1.2), not significant.
  - Female, unmet health: 1.14 (1–1.3).
  - Not in union vs currently married: 1.22 (1.02–1.46).
  - Living alone, unmet health: 1.05 (0.83–1.34), not significant. For unmet food, living alone was 2.55 (2.12–3.06).
  - Age, adjusted: 75–84 "68% more likely" to have unmet need.
  - Decomposition of the rural-urban gap in unmet health need: "education (28.2%), household size (2.32%), and MPC[E]."
- Main limitation (authors):
  - The indicators could be refined.
  - Unmeasured heterogeneity, for example financial dependence and diagnosed conditions.
  - Recall bias, and a cross-sectional design.
  - "The experience of unmet need can be dynamic."
  - Also: subgroup prevalences in Tables 2 and 3 (roughly 7–10%) do not obviously match the 8%/3% in the text. Example: Table 3 prints "Female | 9.61 | 7.37".
- Quality: peer-reviewed, using a government survey (LASI).
- Stages: S0, S1.

**B4. Agrawal G, Keshri K. (2014). Morbidity patterns and health care seeking behavior among older widows in India. *PLoS ONE*, 9(4):e94295.** This is an older study, kept because it is the main national analysis of widows.

- Opened: full text.
- Sample size: 10,111 widows aged 60+, drawn from 34,831 persons aged 60+, with 3,267 widowers used for comparison. Age band: 60+.
- Setting: national, rural and urban. Community survey (NSS 60th round, 2004), cross-sectional.
- Key findings, as printed:
  - "morbidity prevalence was 13% greater among older widows compared to older widowers," with 337 vs 299 per 1000.
  - "older widows living in urban areas had 14% greater likelihood of accessing health care services compared to older widows living in rural areas at 10% level of significance."
  - "Oldest-old widows in age 70+ were 15% (p<0.10) less likely" to seek care.
  - SC/ST and OBC widows were "less likely to seek treatment … by 42% (p<0.001) and 26% (p<0.001)."
  - "Older widows living with children and other relatives had 49% (p<0.001) greater likelihood of seeking health treatment … compared with those living alone."
  - "Older widows of MPCE class5 were 2.9 times (p<0.001) more likely to seek treatment … compared with older widows of MPCE class1."
  - Economic dependence: the text says "economically dependent older widows had greater likelihood of seeking treatment." Table 3, however, prints partially dependent 1.10 (0.82–1.49) and fully dependent 1.12 (0.88–1.42), and neither interval excludes 1.
- Main limitation: no explicit limitations section was found. From the design:
  - The data are from 2004, before NRHM, PMJAY and NPHCE.
  - Morbidity is self-reported.
  - The design is cross-sectional.
  - The treatment-seeking model covers widows only.
- Quality: peer-reviewed, using a government survey (NSS).
- Stages: S0, S10.

**B6. Jeyalakshmi K, Rao M, Shashidhara YN, Thunga G, Ravishankar N, Sudhakar C, Sanatombi Devi E. (2023). Determinants of medication non-adherence among the elderly with co-existing hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus in rural areas of Udupi District in Karnataka, India. *Patient Preference and Adherence*, 17:1641–1656.**
- URL: https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784
- Opened: full text, via the Dovepress page.
- Sample size: 360 (72 from each of 5 PHC clusters). Age band: 60+, mean age 69 (SD 7.464), 61.9% (223) female.

**B9. Das S, Gnanavel P, Smanla S, Saikia AM, Mishra S, Khare S, et al., Lundborg CS. (2025). Polypharmacy and self-medication among older adults in Indian urban communities—a cross-sectional study. *Scientific Reports*, 15:4062.**
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11791031/
- Opened: full text.
- Sample size: 600 (100 per city). Age band: ≥60, mean 72 ± 3.6 years, 58.8% female.
- Setting: urban field-practice areas in New Delhi, Chennai, Kolkata, Ujjain, Patna and Guwahati. Community setting, door-to-door survey, systematic random sampling, November 2022 to January 2024. Appropriateness assessed with STOPP/START v3.
- Key findings, as printed:

    - "The prevalence of self-medication was 19.7% (95% CI 16.6–23.1%)."

  - Traditional and complementary medicines: 164 (27.3%) used them concomitantly. Of these, 80 (48.8%) used solid oral formulations "without names or labels."

  - The study was not powered for city comparisons or for risk factors.
  - The definition of polypharmacy excluded non-oral and non-solid formulations, so it is an underestimate.
  - People with "severe cognitive or hearing deficits" were excluded.
  - The questionnaire was not fully validated for older adults.
  - Interviewer and response bias are possible.

#### Barriers by stage: how consistent and how large (each figure belongs to one study and is not combined)

**Distance and transport (S0/S1/S12): reported in B1, B5 and B11.**
- Average distances in LASI were 14.54 km for outpatient care and 43.62 km for inpatient care [B1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579).
- Outpatient use was 73% within 10 km, falling to 10% at 30 km or more [B1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579).
- Only 5% arrived for admission by ambulance [B1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579).
- Qualitatively, transport led to "missed appointments, delayed care" [B5](https://doi.org/10.1080/17441692.2023.2274438), and a 1–2 hour journey to hospital for people with dementia [B11](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-025-02408-3).

**Cost and out-of-pocket spending (S10): reported in B4, B6, B7, B8 and B13.**
- 46.51% catastrophic health expenditure at the 10% threshold [B13](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55142-1).
- 66.3% of poor adherers cited non-affordability [B8](https://doaj.org/article/36809bc322974bda88bdd7e80765a08a).
- High medication cost correlated with adherence, r = −0.237 [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
- Children paid treatment costs for 408 (51%) [B7](https://doi.org/10.7860/JCDR/2018/29729.11025).
- Widows in the top MPCE class were 2.9 times as likely as the bottom class to seek treatment [B4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094295).

**Medicine-taking (S8): reported in B6, B7, B8, B9 and B10.**
- Non-adherence: 55.6% [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784); 38.6% [B7](https://doi.org/10.7860/JCDR/2018/29729.11025); 44.63% adherent in [B8](https://doaj.org/article/36809bc322974bda88bdd7e80765a08a).
- Forgetting: 31.1% [B7](https://doi.org/10.7860/JCDR/2018/29729.11025); 67.5% of poor adherers [B8](https://doaj.org/article/36809bc322974bda88bdd7e80765a08a). Poor memory: χ² = 19.210 [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
- Supply failure: 72.3% [B8](https://doaj.org/article/36809bc322974bda88bdd7e80765a08a).
- Polypharmacy: 33.7% in the community [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2).
- Potentially inappropriate medication: 87.3% of inpatients by Beers 2012 [B10](https://doi.org/10.4103/2229-3485.140562); 28.8% of community prescriptions by STOPP [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2).
- Self-medication: 19.7% [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2).

**Waiting, crowding, navigating facilities (S3/S4): reported in B3 and B11.**
- In public facilities, 9.5% of OPD users rated waiting time negatively [B3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.puhip.2024.100541) (45+).
- Carers described waits of 2 to 4 hours and long walks from the gate to the OPD [B11](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-025-02408-3).
- No study measured registration, multiple counters, or report collection (S3/S6/S7) for older adults specifically. See Gaps.

**Consultation and communication (S5): reported in B3, B11 and B12.**
- 3.8% of inpatients rated clarity of explanation negatively [B3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.puhip.2024.100541).
- Dementia carers valued "spending ample time" and reported "poor communication" [B11](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-025-02408-3).
- 20.5% reported hearing difficulty, of whom about 5% used hearing aids [B12](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-21467-y).

**Follow-up and continuity (S9): reported in B5 and B11, both qualitative only.**
- Missed appointments due to transport [B5](https://doi.org/10.1080/17441692.2023.2274438).
- Frequent rotation of doctors [B11](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-025-02408-3).

### Inferences
- Cost turns up at more care-journey stages than any other barrier: it affects whether people seek care (B4, B13), whether they keep taking medicines (B6, B8), and who pays (B7). It is probably the most cross-cutting barrier in this evidence base. The magnitudes cannot be pooled, because each study measures something different.
- Medicine supply in the public system (72.3% in B8) may matter as much as patient forgetfulness. That figure comes from a single rural clinic, so it is a signal, not a national estimate.
- The two strands of evidence measure different things. LASI patient-experience ratings show few negative reports (B3), while qualitative accounts describe long waits (B11). The survey measures satisfaction; the interviews describe the burden. Low expectations among older adults, which the B3 authors raise themselves, may explain part of the gap.
- Studies of adherence and of potentially inappropriate medication tend to exclude people with dementia, severe hearing loss, or who cannot read (B7, B9). Published rates may therefore understate problems in the groups most at risk.

### Gaps
- Not found: an Indian study that measures registration queues, multiple counters, getting tests done, or collecting reports (S3, S6, S7) specifically among older adults. B3 covers waiting time only, and its age band is 45+.
- Not found: a peer-reviewed Indian study that quantifies loss to follow-up among older adults in NCD clinics (NP-NCD/NPCDCS). Searches returned all-age continuum-of-care studies only.
- Not found: an Indian study measuring consultation length, or being unable to read prescription labels, among older adults. The one health-literacy and label-comprehension study found (Healthcare, Basel, 2026) was set in Saudi Arabia and was excluded.
- Not found: a study that quantifies the need for an escort or attendant to reach care. The evidence is qualitative only (B11), and B5 was read as abstract only.
- B8's sample size and adherence measure were NOT FOUND, because the full text was not accessible.

## Which findings apply to older women, the rural poor, people living alone, or people with dementia or sensory loss?

### Takeaway
Rural residence and poverty are the most consistently disadvantaged groups. They have more unmet need, travel further, have higher catastrophic spending, and use fewer sensory aids (B1, B2, B12, B13). The evidence for living alone points in two directions. It is linked to self-medication (aOR 4.5, B9) and to lower care-seeking among widows (B4), but it was not significant for unmet health need in LASI (B2) or for adherence in Udupi (B6). For women, findings are mixed (see the contradictions section). Dementia evidence is qualitative only (B11). Sensory loss shows a large treatment gap for hearing (B12), but no Indian study links sensory loss to what happens inside the consultation.

### Cited Findings

**Older women and widows**
- Female sex was associated with unmet health need, OR 1.14 (1–1.3); being not in union, OR 1.22 (1.02–1.46) — [B2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-16126-4).
- Widows had morbidity "13% greater" than widowers, at 337 vs 299 per 1000 — [B4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094295).
- Among widows, the oldest-old (70+) were "15% (p<0.10) less likely" to seek care, and SC/ST widows were 42% less likely — [B4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094295).
- Women used hearing aids at 4.4% and visual aids at 50.7% (Table 2) — [B12](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-21467-y).
- At the 10% threshold, the catastrophic-expenditure headcount was 43.33 for females and 47.92 for males — [B13](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55142-1).
- Females were less likely than males to rate waiting time negatively, AOR 0.59 (0.39–0.89) — [B3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.puhip.2024.100541) (45+).
- Gender was not associated with adherence (χ² = 0.059) — [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
- The decline in use with distance "was more pronounced" for women; no numbers are given in the text — [B1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579).

**Rural and poor**
- Unmet need was 8% rural vs 3% urban. Rural residence was not significant after adjustment (1.05, 0.91–1.2). Education explains 28.2% of the gap — [B2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-16126-4).
- "Around 19% of rural older adults had to travel at least 60 km to avail in-patient care," compared with 10% of urban older adults — [B1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579).
- The poorest quintile spends 24.8% of consumption on health. The catastrophic-expenditure headcount was 55.54 for the poorest vs 39.43 for the richest, and 49.79 rural vs 41.22 urban — [B13](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55142-1).
- Among the poor, 35.2% used visual aids and 3.3% used hearing aids — [B12](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-21467-y).
- Rural widows: urban widows had "14% greater likelihood" of accessing care (p<0.10) — [B4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094295).
- In a rural clinic, irregular supply from the government pharmacy was cited by 72.3% and non-affordability by 66.3% — [B8](https://doaj.org/article/36809bc322974bda88bdd7e80765a08a).

**People living alone**
- Self-medication was associated with "staying alone", aOR 4.5 (95% CI 2.4–6.6) — [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2).
- Widows living with children or relatives had "49% (p<0.001) greater likelihood of seeking health treatment" than widows living alone — [B4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094295).
- Living alone was not significant for unmet health need, 1.05 (0.83–1.34). It was significant for unmet food need, 2.55 (2.12–3.06) — [B2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-16126-4).
- Living status was not significantly associated with adherence, and only 5.6% (n=20) lived alone — [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
- Living alone was linked to a steeper fall in use with distance (no number given in the text) — [B1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579). It was also linked to more reported vision difficulty — [B12](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-21467-y).

**Dementia and cognition**
- Carers of people with dementia reported waits of "two hours, three or four hours to meet the doctor", during which "my mother would not sit patiently." They also described long walks on campus, transport costs, and "frequent rotation of doctors" — [B11](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-025-02408-3).
- In a rural sample, poor memory (χ² = 19.210) and more than one functional impairment (χ² = 25.013) were associated with adherence — [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
- People with dementia were excluded in B7, and people with severe cognitive deficits in B9 — [B7](https://doi.org/10.7860/JCDR/2018/29729.11025); [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2).

**Sensory loss**
- 59% reported vision difficulty, and 53.9% of them used visual aids. 20.5% reported hearing difficulty, and about 5% of them used hearing aids — [B12](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-21467-y).
- Poor vision was associated with medication adherence (χ² = 4.394) — [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
- People with severe hearing deficits were excluded from the multi-city medication study — [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2).

**Family dependence**
- "About 408 (51%) expressed that their treatment cost was taken care of by their children" — [B7](https://doi.org/10.7860/JCDR/2018/29729.11025).
- Good family support, including financial support, correlated with adherence, r = 0.185 — [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
- Family members and friends were the information source for 18 (15.3%) of those who self-medicated — [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2).

### Inferences
- Living alone appears to matter most where someone has to step in: deciding to seek care (B4) and medicine safety (B9). It appears to matter less for the crude LASI measure of unmet need (B2). This may be because "poor health and no visit in 365 days" misses partial or delayed care. That explanation is an inference, not a tested finding.
- Most older people (51% in B7) depended on their children to pay for treatment, and family support correlated with adherence (B6). Together these suggest that the family member, not the older adult, often controls the payment step (S10). No study measured this directly for appointments or for collecting medicines.
- Among the subgroups, the gap between need and treatment is clearest for hearing loss (B12: about 5% of those with hearing difficulty used aids). Hearing loss during consultations is plausibly a barrier to understanding instructions, but this is inferred, not measured, in Indian data.

### Gaps
- No Indian quantitative study of people with dementia was found that covers navigating facilities, medicine management, or follow-up. B11 and Hossien et al. 2017 are qualitative. Hossien et al. (35 caregivers, NIMHANS; abstract only; *Asian J Psychiatr* 30:59–64, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajp.2017.07.002) report that "caregivers were poorly informed about the illness details such as diagnosis, course and outcome." It is outside the 13 entries because only the abstract was opened.
- No LASI-era (2017–18) analysis of widows specifically was opened. B4 uses 2004 data.
- No study was found that compares adherence for older women and men while controlling for financial dependence.
- No Indian study was found that measures communication failure during consultations caused by hearing or vision loss.

## Where do studies contradict each other? (Gaps and contradictions)

### Takeaway
Two kinds of contradiction appear. Some are between studies: women's disadvantage, whether living alone matters, how much waiting and communication are a burden, and how common potentially inappropriate medication is. Others are within a single paper: B1's text vs its table, B2's text vs its tables, B4's text vs its table, B6 on education, B7's 38.6% vs 38.7%, and B10's 87.3% vs 92.5%. The report writer should cite the specific printed value and avoid quoting any single "headline" figure without its context.

### Cited Findings

**Contradictions between studies**
- **Are women disadvantaged?**
  - In LASI, female sex was associated with unmet health need, OR 1.14 (1–1.3) [B2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-16126-4).
  - In NSS 2017–18, however, the catastrophic-expenditure headcount was lower for females (43.33) than males (47.92) [B13](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55142-1).
  - Females were less likely to rate waiting time negatively, AOR 0.59 [B3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.puhip.2024.100541).
  - Gender was not associated with adherence, χ² = 0.059 [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
  - Lower spending and fewer complaints among women may reflect forgone care or lower expectations, not better access. This is an inference; none of these studies tested it.
- **Does living alone matter?**
  - It was strongly associated in [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2) (self-medication, aOR 4.5) and in [B4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094295). In B4, widows "living with children and other relatives had 49% (p<0.001) greater likelihood of seeking health treatment … compared with those living alone."
  - It was not significant in [B2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-16126-4) (unmet health, 1.05, 0.83–1.34) or in [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784) (adherence).
- **Economic dependence: help or harm?**
  - The B4 text says economically dependent widows were more likely to seek treatment, but its Table 3 odds ratios are 1.10 (0.82–1.49) and 1.12 (0.88–1.42), both non-significant [B4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094295).
  - Separately, family financial support correlated positively with adherence, r = 0.185 [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
  - Dependence may enable care when the family pays and constrain it otherwise. No Indian study separates the two.
- **Waiting time and communication: rarely a problem, or a major burden?**
  - In LASI, outpatient negative ratings ranged from 4.6% (waiting time) down to 2.2% (respectful treatment), and "over 70 %" rated care Good or Very Good [B3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.puhip.2024.100541).
  - This is contradicted by qualitative accounts of 2–4 hour waits, poor communication and "frequent rotation of doctors" [B11](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-025-02408-3).
  - The B3 authors themselves flag social desirability bias and low expectations.
- **Rural disadvantage: does it survive adjustment?**
  - Rural older adults had higher unmet need (8% vs 3%), but this was not significant after adjustment, OR 1.05 (0.91–1.2). The gap is mostly explained by education, at 28.2% [B2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-16126-4).
  - Rural disadvantage persisted in distance [B1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579) and in catastrophic spending, 49.79 vs 41.22 [B13](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55142-1).
- **Prevalence of potentially inappropriate medication: 28.8% or 87.3%?**
  - 28.8% of prescriptions in urban community dwellers (STOPP v3, 60+) [B9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84627-2).
  - 87.3% of patients among inpatients (Beers 2012, 65+) [B10](https://doi.org/10.4103/2229-3485.140562).
  - The two studies differ in setting, tool, age band and denominator (prescriptions vs patients), so they are not comparable.
- **Main reason for non-adherence.**
  - Forgetting was the leading reason in a tertiary OPD, 31.1% [B7](https://doi.org/10.7860/JCDR/2018/29729.11025).
  - Irregular government pharmacy supply (72.3%) ranked above forgetting (67.5%) at a rural health centre [B8](https://doaj.org/article/36809bc322974bda88bdd7e80765a08a).
  - In rural Udupi, the number of prescribed drugs was not significant, while knowledge and memory were [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
  - B7 excluded patients who could not read or write English or Kannada, while B8 found literacy associated with poor adherence. So the importance of literacy depends on who was allowed into the sample.

**Contradictions within a single paper (cite with care)**
- B1: the text says 67% of urban outpatients were within 10 km, but Table 1 prints 87.2 for urban and 67.1 for rural. A corrigendum exists, and what it changed was NOT FOUND — [B1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2025.100579).
- B2: the text gives 8% rural vs 3% urban, but Table 3 subgroup values are around 7–10% — [B2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-16126-4).
- B3: "9.1 % (8.1 % - 'bad' and 1.4%-'very bad')" does not add up as printed — [B3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.puhip.2024.100541).
- B6: education is reported as significant in the abstract but as not significant in the Results (χ² = 9.440, p = 0.05). Adherent n=157 plus non-adherent n=200 does not equal 360 — [B6](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S380784).
- B7: non-adherence is 309 (38.6%) in the abstract and 309 (38.7%) in the body — [B7](https://doi.org/10.7860/JCDR/2018/29729.11025).
- B10: 590 (87.3%) in the Results vs "92.5%" in the Discussion — [B10](https://doi.org/10.4103/2229-3485.140562).

### Inferences
- The strongest evidence, from large national samples with exact numbers, is on distance (B1), unmet need (B2), patient experience (B3) and catastrophic expenditure (B13). Evidence on the facility-navigation steps (S3, S4, S6, S7) and on follow-up (S9) is thin and mostly qualitative.
- Adherence studies are small, set in single sites in Karnataka or West Bengal, and use different tools (Hill-Bone, MMAS-8, unspecified). Their prevalence figures, from 38.6% non-adherent to 55.6% non-adherent, should not be averaged.

### Gaps
- **Facility navigation (S3/S4/S6/S7):** no Indian older-adult study was found that quantifies registration, multiple counters, getting tests done, or collecting reports.
- **Follow-up loss (S9):** no Indian older-adult study was found that gives a loss-to-follow-up rate.
- **Consultation (S5):** no Indian data were found on consultation length for older adults, or on hearing or vision loss during the consultation itself.
- **Labels and health literacy (S8/S5):** no Indian study was found that measures older adults' inability to read medicine labels, or their health literacy, outside digital-literacy work, which is excluded from this scope.
- **Escort or attendant need:** no Indian study was found that quantifies escort needs. Only qualitative mentions exist (B11).
- **Dated data:** B4 (2004 NSS), B10 (2010 records), B12 (2011 BKPAI) and B7 (2013–15) predate PMJAY and current NP-NCD drug-supply arrangements, so their figures may not reflect the current system.
- **Opened as abstract only:** B5 and B8. Sample size, adherence measure and stated limitations for B8 are NOT FOUND.
- **B1 article type:** B1 is published as a "Comment", not as a full original research article. The writer may want to treat it with that caveat.
