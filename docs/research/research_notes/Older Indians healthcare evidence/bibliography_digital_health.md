# Older adults (60+) in India and digital health: annotated bibliography of empirical studies

Scope: India only; digital and remote care (telemedicine, teleconsultation including eSanjeevani, ABHA/ABDM, online OPD registration, mHealth, digital payments, family members acting as digital proxies). General access barriers and medication adherence are covered by another researcher.

Method note: All 11 studies below were opened. Full text was read for B1–B11, except the final ~25% of the B11 full-text XML, which was not read. Numbers are given exactly as printed. Where an abstract and the body disagree, both are shown. "NOT FOUND" means the detail was not in the text I read. Nothing is rounded, extrapolated, or combined across studies. One government administrative statistic (G1) is listed separately because it is not a study.

Stage tags: S1 Find doctor · S2 Book · S3 Register · S4 Wait/navigate · S5 Consultation · S6 Tests · S7 Reports · S8 Medicines · S9 Follow-ups · S10 Payments/insurance · S11 Digital health IDs/teleconsult · S12 Emergencies · S0 Other.

## Annotated bibliography

### B1. Rafi 2025: AI-enabled telemedicine among older adults, Aligarh (UP)
- **Citation:** Rafi S. (2025). Artificial intelligence and telemedicine in elderly healthcare: A mixed-methods study. *BMC Geriatrics*, 26(1):71. PMID 41402744; PMCID PMC12822012. (The record lists one author.)
- **Sample size:** Survey n = 200 adults aged ≥60 (mean age 68.2, SD 6.4, range 60–85). Interviews with 20 older adults and 10 healthcare professionals.
- **Setting:** Aligarh, Uttar Pradesh. Cross-sectional mixed methods, January–June 2025. The urban/rural split of the whole sample: NOT FOUND. Among adopters, 61% were urban and 39% semi-urban (χ² = 12.5, p < 0.01).
- **Key findings (as printed):**
  - 68% were aware of AI-enabled health tools and 52% used at least one: wearable monitoring devices 32%, **teleconsultation 28%**, AI medication-reminder apps 21%, social/companion robots 9%.
  - Barriers: equity and accessibility 55%; "digital divide (low digital literacy, dependence on family)" 49%; trust and human connection (including preference for personal contact) 46%; cost 45%; ethical concerns 42%; lack of trust in AI decisions 37%.
  - Perceived benefits: convenient access 61%; better chronic-disease monitoring 54%; improved medication adherence 42%.
  - Family support correlated with perceived empowerment (r = 0.42, p < 0.05; β = 0.36, p = 0.004).
  - Quotes: "Teleconsultation saves time, but sometimes the app freezes, and I feel helpless until someone younger helps me." "I can check my blood pressure easily, but I still need my grandson to explain the readings."
- **Main limitation:** Single region, self-report, cross-sectional. The survey counts teleconsultation as one kind of "AI-enabled tool", so 28% is not a clean telemedicine-use measure. No satisfaction data. The Results compare urban with semi-urban, but the Discussion says urban vs rural.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-025-06872-y · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12822012/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Stages:** S5, S8, S9, S11.

### B2. Rasekaba et al. 2022 (DAHLIA): telehealth readiness of rural older people, Mysuru (Karnataka)
- **Citation:** Rasekaba TM, Pereira P, Rani G VR, Johnson R, McKechnie R, Blackberry I. (2022). Exploring Telehealth Readiness in a Resource Limited Setting: Digital and Health Literacy among Older People in Rural India (DAHLIA). *Geriatrics (Basel)*, 7(2):28.
- **Sample size:** Survey n = 150, aged over 65 (median 71, range 65–99; 62.7% male; 67.4% primary education or less). Three focus groups (8, 9 and 8 participants) plus a community forum of 33.
- **Setting:** Rural Mysore and Suttur village, Karnataka (JSS Hospital catchment). Mixed-methods cross-sectional study, 5–19 September 2019 (before COVID).
- **Key findings (as printed):**
  - Phones: 73 participants had a standard phone, 13 a smartphone, and 63 (42.0%) none. The abstract gives mobile phone ownership as 50%.
  - Smartphone with internet: 8.7% (13). Internet at home: 9.3% (14).
  - Digital literacy: 11% (abstract). The body gives 10.7% (16) who had used the internet for shopping, banking or health purposes.
  - Used the internet to contact or find health providers: 10.7% (16) in Table 2; the abstract says "less than 10%".
  - Health literacy across domains: adequate reading 3.3%, learning about conditions 23.3%, completing forms 27.3%. eHEALS median 24.0.
  - Qualitative: children help older adults use technology and find health information ("My son will browse the internet for information regarding health issues…"). Participants valued face-to-face consultations.
- **Main limitation:** No multivariable analysis. Focus groups ran in English through a translator. Abstract and tables disagree on phone ownership and on online contact with providers. Willingness to use telehealth was not measured.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/geriatrics7020028 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8938771/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Stages:** S1, S11, S0.

### B3. Patel et al. 2023/2024: digital health literacy, rural vs urban Telangana
- **Citation:** Patel S, Sreelal BS, Kalyani S, Joy SG, Pravalika T, Ramya T, Khan S, Archana V, Gandhi AP. (2023, online 31 Oct; issue 2024). Digital health literacy among the ageing population: A comparative cross-sectional study between rural and urban Telangana, India. *Medical Journal Armed Forces India*, 80(3):313–319.
- **Sample size:** n = 318 (159 urban, 159 rural), **aged 45 and above**. Data for the 60+ subgroup: NOT FOUND in the text reviewed.
- **Setting:** Hyderabad, Telangana: two urban primary health centres and one rural. Interviewer-administered cross-sectional survey, March 2023, consecutive sampling.
- **Key findings (as printed; urban vs rural):**
  - Good digital health literacy: 20.4% overall (rural 20.1%, urban 20.8%, p = 0.889).
  - Smartphone: 36.5% vs 19.5%. No working mobile phone: 17.6% vs 42.1% (p < 0.001).
  - Internet at home: 30.8% vs 8.2%. WhatsApp use: 33.3% vs 15.7%. Confidence using the internet: 19.5% vs 5.0%.
  - Online activities: paying bills or banking 16.4% vs 3.1% (p < 0.001); contacting or finding a healthcare provider 10.7% vs 2.5% (p = 0.003); getting health information 12.6% vs 2.5%; ordering or refilling prescriptions 3.1% vs 0.6% (p = 0.099).
  - Determinants of digital health literacy: computer use, education, and mobile phone ownership.
  - Not measured: telemedicine use, online appointments, help from family.
- **Main limitation:** Mixed 45+ sample with no 60+ breakdown. Recruited at primary health centres, so likely biased towards people who seek care. eHEALS not validated for Indian settings.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mjafi.2023.09.006 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11117001/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Stages:** S1, S8, S10, S11.

### B4. Sood et al. 2025: national eSanjeevani utilisation analysis
- **Citation:** Sood S, Lal K, Bhatia M, Kapoor G, Singh S, Kaushish RK, Bisht D, Sharma VK, Chakraborty S, Shete M, Solanki NK, Naskar A, Sharma M, Subramaniyan EU, Waikar S, Verma N. (2025). Adoption and utilization of India's eSanjeevani national telemedicine service. *Oxford Open Digital Health*, 3:oqaf025.
- **Sample size:** Aggregated programme data from C-DAC. "Over 163 million consultations from January 2020 to September 2023." The unit is consultations, not people.
- **Setting:** National, all platforms. Descriptive secondary analysis covering November 2019–September 2023. The age-group figures are labelled April 2020–September 2023.
- **Key finding for older adults (as printed):** "Children (0–14 years) and older adults (60+ years) used the platform less, though their usage also increased in 2021." The 60+ counts and shares appear only in Figures 13–14 and are not printed in the text (exact values NOT FOUND).
- **Main limitation:** Administrative data with no unique patient identifiers, so unique users, repeat visits and continuity cannot be measured. No consultation-level records. Does not measure outcomes or satisfaction.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1093/oodh/oqaf025 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12558045/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed (analysis of programme data).
- **Stages:** S5, S11.

### B5. Mahala et al. 2024: RCT of telephone teleconsultation vs usual care for 60+, Rishikesh (Uttarakhand)
- **Citation:** Mahala P, Kaeley N, Saxena V, Arora S, Bahurupi Y, Kalyani V. (2024). Efficacy of Teleconsultation Versus Usual Care in Improving Quality of Life Among Geriatric Patients Aged 60 and Above: A Randomized Controlled Trial. *Cureus*, 16(9):e68476.
- **Sample size:** 2,000 enrolled (1,000 per arm), aged over 60. Mean age 68.04 (teleconsultation arm) and 69.70 (usual care).
- **Setting:** A tertiary care hospital in Rishikesh, Uttarakhand, serving a hilly population. Participants were recruited at emergency department triage. Block-randomised trial, September 2021–December 2023. Monthly telephone contact for one year after discharge, with home devices (BP, pulse, glucose, SpO2, weight). **Participants had to already own ISO-certified home monitoring devices** and to speak English or Hindi.
- **Key findings (as printed, Table 3):**
  - Post-intervention total quality-of-life score: 56.19 vs 50.92 (P < 0.001), with n = 416 vs n = 399.
  - Adjusted change in total QoL (Table 4): 8.61 vs 3.36 (P < 0.001).
  - Abstract values differ slightly from the tables (e.g., 14.49 vs Table 14.48 for physical health).
- **Main limitation:** Only 416 of 1,000 (intervention) and 399 of 1,000 (control) appear in the post-intervention analysis, with no explanation despite an intention-to-treat claim. The device-ownership requirement selects better-off patients. Not reported: who operated the phone (patient or family), satisfaction, and technical problems.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.68476 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11446495/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Stages:** S5, S9, S11, S12.

### B6. Mathew et al. 2024: Project Vayoraksha mHealth app for 60+ during COVID-19, Pathanamthitta (Kerala)
- **Citation:** Mathew G, Bava N, Varghese AD, Sushan A, Benjamin AI. (2024). Project *Vayoraksha*: Implementation of novel mHealth technology for healthcare delivery during COVID-19 in geriatric population of Kerala. *Indian Journal of Medical Research*, 159(3&4):289–297.
- **Sample size:** Baseline survey of 4,782 people aged ≥60. App users: n = 452.
- **Setting:** Pathanamthitta Municipality (urban, 32 wards), Kerala. Field operational research pilot, October 2020–July 2021.
- **Key findings (as printed):**
  - Uptake funnel: smartphone access 2,383 (49.8%) → reached by the field campaign 1,257 (52.7%) → downloaded the app 750 (59.6%) → used services 452 (60.3%).
  - Among users: 56 (12.3%) registered a need, of which 46 (82.1%) were medical, mostly chronic-disease medicines. **Teleconsultation was used by 3 (0.6%)**; tele-counselling by 26 (5.7%).
  - Needs met: "more than 70 per cent" (abstract) vs "nearly 75 per cent" (Results).
  - Quarantine breaks: 101 (22.3%), most often to buy particular brands of medicines (64, 14.1%).
  - Users were more often aged 60–69, educated, living with family, and had chronic morbidity.
  - Any family member could install and use the app on the older person's behalf, and student volunteers helped run the campaign. How much use was by proxy: NOT FOUND (not quantified).
  - One-to-one phone outreach gave suboptimal results, so the team switched to a door-to-door field campaign.
- **Main limitation:** Pilot with no comparison group, short duration, and limited publicity. Satisfaction and proxy use not measured.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.25259/IJMR_62_23 · https://ijmr.org.in/project-vayoraksha-implementation-of-novel-mhealth-technology-for-healthcare-delivery-during-covid-19-in-geriatric-population-of-kerala/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Stages:** S5, S8, S11, S0.

### B7. Mukku et al. 2021: NIMHANS geriatric video aftercare clinic, Bengaluru
- **Citation:** Mukku SSR, Manjunatha N, Kumar CN, Sivakumar PT, Math SB. (2021). Video consultations from tele aftercare clinic: An early experience from an Indian geriatric psychiatry service. *Indian Journal of Psychiatry*, 63(1):102–103. (Letter to the editor.)
- **Sample size:** 30 older adults; 112 video consultations (mean 3.73, SD 2.36, per patient). Mean age 64.57 (SD 8.50). No age cut-off stated.
- **Setting:** NIMHANS Tele-Medicine Centre and Geriatric Clinic, Bengaluru, Karnataka. Direct-to-patient video follow-up after an in-person evaluation, March 2018–May 2019. Patients: urban 83.3%, mean education 13.73 years, married 96.7%, retired 60%.
- **Key findings (as printed):**
  - Reasons for choosing video follow-up: same doctor 100%, affordability 100%, convenience 100%, easy access to technology 96.7%, severe symptoms 66.7%, mobility issues 60%, lack of personnel 36.7%, financial constraints 16.7%.
  - Most patients could not use digital technology on their own; a close family member facilitated the video consultation and was present while the psychiatrist reviewed the patient. No count is given.
- **Main limitation:** Urban, highly educated, middle/upper-SES patients. Descriptive letter with no satisfaction or outcome measures. Rural feasibility not evaluated, as the authors state.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.4103/psychiatry.IndianJPsychiatry_719_19 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8106428/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed (letter).
- **Stages:** S5, S9, S11.

### B8. Ullas et al. 2021: COVID-era telemedicine for NCD patients, Kerala (mixed ages; 61+ subgroup)
- **Citation:** Ullas S, Pradeep M, Surendran S, Ravikumar A, Bastine AM, Prasad A, Mohan A. (2021). Telemedicine During the COVID-19 Pandemic: A Paradigm Shift in Non-Communicable Disease Management? A Cross-Sectional Survey from a Quaternary-Care Center in South India. *Patient Preference and Adherence*, 15:2715–2723.
- **Sample size:** 220 analysed (of 289 recorded). Mixed ages: **61 and above = 40 (18.18%)**; mean age 47.37 ± 13.59. Results by age: NOT FOUND (no age-stratified results).
- **Setting:** A quaternary-care hospital in Kerala, across seven departments including Geriatric Medicine. Online Google Forms survey, January 2021. Teleconsultations ran on Zoom video.
- **Key findings (as printed):**
  - Telemedicine for routine consultations: 85 (38.63%).
  - Comfortable with telemedicine: 84 (38.18%) agreed. Whether telemedicine is an adequate substitute: modal answer "not sure" (n = 72, 32.72%).
  - Preference: 135 chose in-person consultations.
  - Satisfied with current routine care: 111 (50.45%), not broken down by modality.
  - Fear of COVID-19 was the most common reason for difficulty attending (44.09%, n = 97). In-person visits fell from 3.47 (2019) to 1.56 (2020) per year (p < 0.001).
- **Main limitation:** Mixed ages with no older-adult breakdown, single centre, and internal inconsistencies (the 0–20 age band conflicts with the over-18 inclusion criterion). Inference: an online survey cannot reach patients without internet access.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S332636 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8667749/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Stages:** S5, S8, S9, S11.

### B9. Banerjee et al. 2021: physicians' experience of remote dementia care in lockdown, South India
- **Citation:** Banerjee D, Vajawat B, Varshney P, Rao TSS. (2021). Perceptions, Experiences, and Challenges of Physicians Involved in Dementia Care During the COVID-19 Lockdown in India: A Qualitative Study. *Frontiers in Psychiatry*, 11:615758.
- **Sample size:** 148 physicians (66 psychiatrists, 32 neurologists, 50 general physicians). **No patients or caregivers were interviewed**; findings about people living with dementia and their families are physicians' reports.
- **Setting:** Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Kerala. Qualitative virtual interviews, April–June 2020.
- **Key findings (as printed; percentages are shares of responses):**
  - 91% reported difficulty with virtual cognitive assessment ("It was indeed very difficult to apply scales over phone/video").
  - 95% cited lack of therapeutic rapport. "Most virtual consults were on a proxy." The authors worry that patients' voices were "unheard".
  - 85% reported caregiver burnout and stress; caregivers sometimes adjusted sedating medicines themselves.
  - 67% found the telemedicine guidelines ambiguous.
  - Physicians were surprised that rural clients were comfortable with video ("They were quite tech-savvy, it surprised me"). Some caregivers resisted "digital interventions".
- **Main limitation:** Physicians only, South India only. The authors were themselves clinicians in the same situation. Hearing and vision limits are discussed as a concern but were not measured.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.615758
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Stages:** S5, S8, S9, S11.

### B10. Sharan et al. 2026: ABHA awareness and registration among outpatients, Ranchi (Jharkhand)
- **Citation:** Sharan E, Singh B, Singh SB. (2026). Awareness, Registration Status, and Perceived Barriers to Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA) Adoption Among Patients in Jharkhand: A Cross-Sectional Study. *Cureus*, 18(10):e117541.
- **Sample size:** n = 400 adults aged ≥20. Oldest category printed: **"50 and above" = 110 (27.5%)**. There is no 60+ category. Age-specific awareness and registration numbers appear only in Figure 3 (not printed in text: NOT FOUND).
- **Setting:** OPDs of Rajendra Institute of Medical Sciences (government tertiary hospital), Ranchi, Jharkhand. Convenience sample, interviews in Hindi or English, May–July 2026.
- **Key findings (as printed):**
  - Heard of ABHA: 187 (46.8%). Knew an account can be created online or through an app: 64 (16.0%). **Personally created an ABHA: 24 (6.0%)**; unsure 40 (10.0%).
  - High perceived barriers: 295 (73.8%).
  - Age and knowledge: χ²(4) = 15.795, p = 0.003, with older patients showing lower knowledge. This was the only association that survived Bonferroni correction.
  - Attitudes: 318 (79.5%) said a hospital helpdesk or kiosk would make them more likely to register; 303 (75.8%) would encourage family and friends to register. Family members actively helping with registration was not measured.
- **Main limitation:** Single centre, convenience sample, registration self-reported (not checked against the portal). The barrier scale had α = 0.26. Age recorded only in broad bands.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.117541 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13738156/
- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Stages:** S3, S11.

### B11. Ranjan et al. 2026: ABDM adoption, digital health literacy and satisfaction, Agra (UP)
- **Citation:** Ranjan A, Singh G, Singh H, Singh M. (2026). Adoption, digital health literacy, and patient satisfaction of Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission: an analytical cross-sectional study among outpatient department attendees. *BMC Health Services Research*, 26(1):512. PMID 41787376; PMCID PMC13078079.
- **Sample size:** n = 425 OPD attendees, mixed ages: **60–74 = 54 (12.7%); 75+ = 6 (1.4%)**. Results by age group: NOT FOUND. The only age result is a Spearman correlation between age and digital health literacy (ρ = −0.230, p < 0.001).
- **Setting:** Three government tertiary hospitals in Agra, Uttar Pradesh, with patients from urban and rural areas (split NOT FOUND). Multistage random sampling, September 2024–April 2025. **The questionnaire was given only to patients who had completed ABHA registration.**
- **Key findings (as printed):**
  - Facility data: 129,007 ABHA registrations out of 537,278 OPD visits; "monthly adoption rate of 23.96%".

- **Quality:** Peer-reviewed.

## Key question 1: What share of older Indians have used telemedicine or teleconsultation, and what stopped others?

### Takeaway
None of the sources I opened gives a nationally representative share of older Indians who have used telemedicine. The local figures vary widely and measure different things: 28% teleconsultation among 200 people aged 60+ in Aligarh (counted as an "AI-enabled tool"), 0.6% teleconsultation among 452 older app users in urban Kerala, and 10.7% (urban) vs 2.5% (rural) of people aged 45+ in Telangana using the internet to contact or find a provider. The barriers that recur are not owning a smartphone or having internet access, low digital and health literacy, cost, a preference for in-person or personal contact, distrust, and needing someone younger to operate the device.

### Cited Findings
- Aligarh, 60+ (n = 200): teleconsultation 28%; any AI-enabled tool 52%; barriers: equity and accessibility 55%, digital divide (low literacy, dependence on family) 49%, trust and human connection 46%, cost 45% — [Rafi 2025, BMC Geriatr](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-025-06872-y)
- Urban Kerala, 60+ (baseline n = 4,782): smartphone access 2,383 (49.8%); of 452 app users, teleconsultation 3 (0.6%), tele-counselling 26 (5.7%) — [Mathew 2024, IJMR](https://doi.org/10.25259/IJMR_62_23)
- Rural Karnataka, over 65 (n = 150): smartphone with internet 8.7%; internet at home 9.3%; used the internet to contact or find health providers 10.7% (Table 2) / "less than 10%" (abstract) — [Rasekaba 2022, Geriatrics](https://doi.org/10.3390/geriatrics7020028)
- Telangana, 45+ (n = 318): contacting or finding a healthcare provider online 10.7% urban vs 2.5% rural; no working mobile phone 17.6% urban vs 42.1% rural; smartphone 36.5% vs 19.5% — [Patel 2023/2024, MJAFI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mjafi.2023.09.006)
- National eSanjeevani data: older adults (60+) "used the platform less, though their usage also increased in 2021"; exact values are only in figures — [Sood 2025, OODH](https://doi.org/10.1093/oodh/oqaf025)
- MoHFW (Oct 2026): "16.25 per cent" of eSanjeevani consultations were by people aged 60 and above (a share of consultations, not of people) — [News On AIR, 6 Oct 2026](https://newsonair.gov.in/esanjeevani-achieves-milestone-of-50-cr-teleconsultations-from-across-the-country/)
- Agra OPD (mixed ages; 60+ n = 60): prefer in-person healthcare 85.6%; cost of data plan 81.9%; difficulty using health apps 65.6%; language difficulty 64.0%; hesitant to share OTP 60.0% — [Ranjan 2026, BMC HSR](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14314-7)
- Remote dementia care (physician-reported): 91% reported difficulty with virtual cognitive assessment; 95% lack of therapeutic rapport — [Banerjee 2021, Front Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.615758)

### Inferences
- The figures cannot be pooled. "Used teleconsultation" (B1), "used the app's teleconsultation feature" (B6), "used the internet to contact a provider" (B2, B3) and "share of consultations" (B4, G1) are different measures in different populations.
- Every study that measured device access found that a large share of older people lacked a smartphone or internet access, so for many people the barrier comes before any question of app usability.
- Hearing and vision limits are not measured as barriers in any study I opened. B9 only raises them as a concern, physician-reported, in dementia care.

### Gaps
- No nationally representative estimate of telemedicine use among people aged 60+ was opened. HelpAge India's 2024 report (5,169 elders, 20 cities) reportedly found 1.5% used telehealth, but the PDF links on helpageindia.org returned 404, so the figure is unverified and is not included above.
- Language was measured only in a mixed-age sample (B11: 64.0%). Distrust was measured only as "trust in AI decisions" / "trust and human connection" (B1).

## Key question 2: What do studies say about older people's awareness and use of ABHA/ABDM, online OPD registration, digital health records and online lab reports?

### Takeaway
The two ABHA studies I opened both use mixed-age hospital samples with few older respondents. Both found low ABHA creation or adoption, and in one, older age was the only factor robustly linked to lower ABHA knowledge. I found no study of older adults and online OPD booking (ORS/AIIMS), digital health records, or online delivery of lab reports.

### Cited Findings
- Ranchi OPD, aged 20+ (oldest band "50 and above" = 110, 27.5%): heard of ABHA 46.8%; knew it can be created online or through an app 16.0%; personally created one 6.0%. Age and knowledge: χ²(4) = 15.795, p = 0.003, older patients lower, the only association robust to Bonferroni correction — [Sharan 2026, Cureus](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.117541)
- Ranchi OPD: 79.5% said a hospital helpdesk or kiosk would make them more likely to register — [Sharan 2026](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.117541)
- Agra, three hospitals: 129,007 ABHA registrations out of 537,278 OPD visits, "monthly adoption rate of 23.96%". Age correlated negatively with digital health literacy (ρ = −0.230, p < 0.001). Over 80% of registrants agreed ABDM made registration easier; 55% felt comfortable with data privacy — [Ranjan 2026, BMC HSR](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14314-7)
- Agra: 48.5% rely on others to use digital devices; 60.0% hesitant to share an OTP — [Ranjan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14314-7)
- Context only (mixed ages, no age data in the abstract, abstract read): at a tertiary hospital in Udupi, Karnataka, 58% of 200 surveyed patients were unaware of the online registration facility; 90.5% were ready to use a digital system — [Amin 2026, BMC HSR](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14299-3)

### Inferences
- In B11 the >80% approval comes only from people who had already registered, so it cannot speak to those who did not register. This includes most older OPD attendees, who were few in the sample.
- B10 and B11 both point to reliance on intermediaries, such as other people for device use or a helpdesk preference. Neither measures who actually completed the registration.

### Gaps
- No study found that reports older-adult-specific figures for online OPD registration (ORS), PHR or ABHA app use, scan-and-share, or lab reports delivered online. A search snippet described an AIIMS Rishikesh online-registration study as covering ages 18–60, but I did not open it, so this is unverified.
- No ABHA study with a 60+ subgroup in printed text was found.

## Key question 3: How do family members or caregivers mediate digital health use, and what happens to older people living alone or without tech-savvy relatives?

### Takeaway
Family mediation comes up in almost every study, mostly as qualitative observation: a family member runs the video call, a grandchild reads the readings, a son searches the internet. It is almost never counted. The one quantitative signal for 60+ is a correlation (family support with empowerment, r = 0.42). No study I opened measured what happens to older people who live alone or have no tech-savvy relatives. Two studies suggest users are more often those living with family.

### Cited Findings
- NIMHANS video clinic (30 older adults): most patients could not use digital technology on their own; a close family member facilitated the consultation. Not quantified — [Mukku 2021, IJP](https://doi.org/10.4103/psychiatry.IndianJPsychiatry_719_19)
- Aligarh 60+: family support correlated with empowerment (r = 0.42, p < 0.05; β = 0.36, p = 0.004). Barrier category "digital divide (low digital literacy, dependence on family)" 49%. Quote: "…I feel helpless until someone younger helps me." — [Rafi 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-025-06872-y)
- Kerala Vayoraksha: any family member could install and use the app on the older person's behalf; users were more often aged 60–69, educated, and **living with family** — [Mathew 2024](https://doi.org/10.25259/IJMR_62_23)
- Rural Karnataka: children help older adults with technology and health information ("My son will browse the internet…") — [Rasekaba 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/geriatrics7020028)
- Remote dementia care: "Most virtual consults were on a proxy"; concern that patients' voices were "unheard"; 85% reported caregiver burnout and stress — [Banerjee 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.615758)
- Agra (mixed ages): 48.5% "rely on others" for help using digital devices — [Ranjan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14314-7)

### Inferences
- The proxy model shifts the consultation from patient to relative. B9 raises this as a concern about the patient's voice, and B7 describes a psychiatrist reviewing the patient with a family member present.
- Two studies (B7, B6) describe users as married or living with family, so the samples likely under-represent older people living alone.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures digital-health outcomes for older people living alone or without a digitally capable relative.
- No study counts how often a consultation, booking or ABHA registration is completed by a proxy rather than the older person.
- The HelpAge India 2025 intergenerational report (reportedly 54% of children and 52% of grandchildren teaching elders digital skills) was not opened. The PARI page did not load its content, so the figures are not included.

## Key question 4: COVID-19-era telemedicine studies with Indian older adults: satisfaction and limitations

### Takeaway
The COVID-era evidence I opened is thin on satisfaction for older adults specifically. The Kerala NCD survey found only 38.18% comfortable with telemedicine, and most respondents preferred in-person care (no age breakdown; 61+ = 18.18% of the sample). The Kerala geriatric mHealth pilot saw almost no teleconsultation use (0.6% of users). Physicians running remote dementia care reported serious assessment difficulties and widespread proxy consultations.

### Cited Findings
- Kerala quaternary centre, January 2021 (n = 220; 61+ = 40): 38.63% used telemedicine for routine consultations; 38.18% comfortable with it; "not sure" was the modal answer (32.72%) on whether it is an adequate substitute; 135 chose in-person; 50.45% satisfied with current routine care — [Ullas 2021, PPA](https://doi.org/10.2147/PPA.S332636)
- Kerala Vayoraksha, Oct 2020–Jul 2021 (60+): of 452 users, teleconsultation 3 (0.6%); needs met "more than 70 per cent" (abstract) / "nearly 75 per cent" (Results); quarantine breaks were most often to buy particular medicine brands (64, 14.1%) — [Mathew 2024](https://doi.org/10.25259/IJMR_62_23)
- South India lockdown, physicians in dementia care (n = 148): 91% difficulty with virtual cognitive assessment; 95% lack of rapport; 67% found guidelines ambiguous — [Banerjee 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.615758)
- Rishikesh RCT (Sept 2021–Dec 2023, 60+): telephone follow-up arm had a higher total QoL score (56.19 vs 50.92, P < 0.001), but only 416 of 1,000 and 399 of 1,000 were analysed — [Mahala 2024, Cureus](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.68476)
- Pre-COVID reference (NIMHANS 2018–19): satisfaction not measured; 100% cited the same doctor, affordability and convenience as reasons for choosing video follow-up — [Mukku 2021](https://doi.org/10.4103/psychiatry.IndianJPsychiatry_719_19)

### Inferences
- No satisfaction score measured specifically in Indian older adults during COVID was found among the opened studies.

### Gaps
- High-satisfaction teleconsultation studies from India (e.g., psychiatric teleconsultation, ~90% satisfied) turned up in search snippets, but they used all-age samples and were not opened, so they are not included.

## Key question 5: Digital literacy and technology-acceptance studies of Indian older adults related to healthcare tasks

### Takeaway
Digital and health literacy for healthcare tasks is low and strongly unequal by place, education and device ownership: about 11% digital literacy among rural people over 65 in Karnataka, and about 20% "good" digital health literacy among people aged 45+ in Telangana. Health tasks done online, such as finding a provider, refilling a prescription or paying bills, are reported by small minorities, and far fewer in rural areas. I could not open any Technology Acceptance Model / UTAUT study with Indian older adults.

### Cited Findings
- Rural Karnataka, over 65: digital literacy 11% (abstract) / 10.7% (body); health literacy domains 3.3%, 23.3%, 27.3%; eHEALS median 24.0 — [Rasekaba 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/geriatrics7020028)
- Telangana 45+: good digital health literacy 20.4% (rural 20.1%, urban 20.8%); ordering or refilling prescriptions online 3.1% urban vs 0.6% rural; paying bills or banking online 16.4% vs 3.1%; confidence using the internet 19.5% vs 5.0% — [Patel 2023/2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mjafi.2023.09.006)
- Agra OPD (mixed ages): mean digital health literacy 2.94 ± 0.72; operational skills 2.38 ± 0.64; age ρ = −0.230 — [Ranjan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14314-7)
- Aligarh 60+: digital literacy and family support predicted perceived empowerment — [Rafi 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-025-06872-y)

### Inferences
- Digital payments for health specifically were not measured in any opened study. The nearest proxies are general "paying bills or banking" (B3) and "shopping, banking or health" combined (B2).

### Gaps
- Not opened, so not included: Dash & Mohanty 2023, a TAM study of older citizens' m-health acceptance in Delhi NCR (record returned 403); an Ajmer hospital-based eHealth literacy study of 306 adults aged 60+ (journal paywall, 402); a Pune mHealth structural-equation study reportedly of 417 adults over 60 (could not be located beyond ResearchGate).

## Key question 6: Where do studies contradict each other?

### Takeaway
The main tension is high approval among people already using a service against low uptake in the wider population. This comes from samples drawn from users (registrants, clinic attendees, app users) set beside population-level access data. Several papers also contradict themselves between abstract and body.

### Cited Findings
- Approval vs uptake (ABHA): over 80% of registrants agreed ABDM made registration easier, while the monthly adoption rate was 23.96% of OPD visits — [Ranjan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14314-7). In Ranchi, 6.0% had created an ABHA — [Sharan 2026](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.117541)
- Access to technology, by sample: 96.7% of NIMHANS video-clinic older adults cited "easy access to technology" (urban 83.3%, mean education 13.73 years) — [Mukku 2021](https://doi.org/10.4103/psychiatry.IndianJPsychiatry_719_19); vs 8.7% with an internet-enabled smartphone among rural people over 65 — [Rasekaba 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/geriatrics7020028)
- Device access vs use: 49.8% of older people in Pathanamthitta had smartphone access, but 0.6% of app users used teleconsultation — [Mathew 2024](https://doi.org/10.25259/IJMR_62_23)
- Rural comfort with video: physicians said rural dementia clients "were quite tech-savvy, it surprised me" — [Banerjee 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.615758); against no working mobile phone in 42.1% of rural people aged 45+ in Telangana — [Patel 2023/2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mjafi.2023.09.006)
- Telemedicine use rates: 28% teleconsultation among people aged 60+ in Aligarh — [Rafi 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-025-06872-y); vs 0.6% of older app users in Kerala — [Mathew 2024](https://doi.org/10.25259/IJMR_62_23) (different measures, settings and years)
- Within-paper inconsistencies: DAHLIA abstract gives 50% phone ownership while Table 2 shows 42.0% with no phone, and "less than 10%" vs 10.7% contacting providers online — [Rasekaba 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/geriatrics7020028). Ranjan's abstract says "58.6% lacked adequate knowledge" while the table shows 58.6% "yes" for knowledge of ABDM — [Ranjan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14314-7). The Rishikesh RCT's abstract and table values differ, and about 58–60% of participants are missing from the post-intervention analysis — [Mahala 2024](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.68476)

### Inferences
- High satisfaction or approval figures in this literature mostly come from people who already use a service. Population-level uptake comes from different studies and settings, so the two kinds of number should not be read against each other as if from one study.

### Gaps
- No single study measures both satisfaction among users and uptake in the eligible older population, so the size of the gap cannot be estimated from these sources.

## Gaps and contradictions

**Gaps**
1. **No nationally representative estimate opened** of telemedicine or eSanjeevani use among people aged 60+. National data (B4, G1) count consultations, not people. HelpAge India 2024 (reported 1.5% telehealth use) and HelpAge India 2025 could not be opened (PDF 404 / page content not served), so they are excluded.
2. **Online OPD booking or registration (ORS/AIIMS), digital lab reports, PHR apps and digital payments for health:** no study with older-adult-specific data was found.
3. **ABHA/ABDM:** both studies use mixed-age samples. B10 has no 60+ band. B11 has 60 respondents aged 60+ and no results by age, and surveyed only registrants.
4. **Proxy use is described but not counted** (B1, B2, B6, B7, B9). No study measures outcomes for older people living alone or without tech-savvy relatives.
5. **Hearing and vision limits, cognitive impairment and language** are not measured as telemedicine barriers among people aged 60+. B9 discusses them speculatively, and language appears only in a mixed-age sample (B11).
6. **Geography and setting bias:** the studies are hospital-, PHC- or clinic-based, from Karnataka, Kerala, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand. Several are urban or highly educated (B7, B6). One survey was online-only (B8), which excludes people with no internet access. No north-east or community-based national sample was found.
7. **Sources from NHSRC, NITI Aayog, Dvara Health, ORF and CIS:** none with older-adult digital-health data was opened in this pass.
8. **Technology-acceptance (TAM/UTAUT) studies** with Indian older adults were identified but not opened (Dash & Mohanty 2023, Delhi NCR; the Pune and Ajmer studies), so none are included.

**Contradictions**
1. High approval among users vs low uptake: B11 (over 80% approval among registrants vs a 23.96% monthly adoption rate) and B10 (6.0% created an ABHA).
2. "Easy access to technology" at 96.7% in an urban, educated clinic sample (B7) vs 8.7% smartphone-with-internet in rural over-65s (B2).
3. Physicians' impression that rural clients were tech-savvy (B9) vs survey evidence of low rural device ownership and confidence (B2, B3).
4. Teleconsultation use of 28% (B1) vs 0.6% (B6): different measures, settings and periods, so not comparable.
5. Within-paper inconsistencies: B2 (phone ownership; online contact with providers), B11 (ABDM knowledge 58.6% "lacked" vs "yes"), B5 (abstract vs tables; about 58–60% missing at follow-up), and B1 (urban vs semi-urban in Results but urban vs rural in Discussion).
