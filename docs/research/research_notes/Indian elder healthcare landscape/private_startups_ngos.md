## Landscape rows: what each player is, whom it serves, journey stages, payer, adoption, gaps (objective)

### Takeaway
I built 30 rows: 7 mass-market apps, 14 elder-care startups and home-care firms, 7 NGO or helpline records and 2 insurers. Three patterns stand out:
- **Mass-market apps.** They are large (Apollo 24/7 has 47M registered users; PharmEasy had ₹5,872 cr revenue in FY25) but publish no age-specific usage data. Their only documented senior-specific features are workarounds outside the app, such as WhatsApp or phone ordering and vernacular teleconsults.
- **Elder-care startups.** They are small and mostly loss-making. Emoha had ₹74 cr revenue against a ₹37 cr loss in FY25. GenWise shut down in Dec 2025 despite claiming 30 lakh users.
- **NGOs.** They run the largest free in-person programmes and are paid for mainly by corporate CSR. HelpAge India had 161 mobile health units delivering 28 lakh treatments in 2024-25, with 60% of its income from corporates.

## Who actually pays in each model, and is the buyer the elder or their children?

### Takeaway
There are four distinct payer models:
1. **Per-transaction consumer payment** for mass-market apps (whoever places the order).
2. **Family or child-paid subscriptions and services** for home-care and elder-care startups. This is documented explicitly only for Samarth (NRI clients in 33+ countries) and implied by Anvayaa's "parent care" positioning.
3. **Elder-paid memberships** at low price points: Khyaal ₹999/year, Emoha's entry plan ₹399. The free-app model (GenWise) failed to monetise.
4. **CSR, donor and government funding** for NGOs and helplines, free to the elder. HelpAge is 60% corporate-funded, and Elderline is government plus Tata Trusts/NSE Foundation.

Employers appear as payers in MediBuddy (corporate health benefits) and in Anvayaa's "Nischint" corporate plan.

### Inferences
- Price points split the market. Elder-paid offers are cheap (₹399–₹999), while hands-on services (₹4,999–₹15,000/month, care homes, senior living) are priced for family or child budgets, especially NRIs.
- Free, mass-reach services for poor and rural elders exist almost only where CSR or government pays (HelpAge MHUs, Elderline).
- Unit economics are weak for app-plus-service startups. Emoha spends ₹1.5 per ₹1 of revenue, and Antara does not expect break-even until FY27–FY28. GenWise's ₹1 cr revenue against a ₹12.1 cr loss shows that a free consumer app for seniors did not cover its costs.

### Gaps
- Emoha, Primus and Antara: no source states what share of buyers are adult children vs the elders themselves.
- No cohort or retention data, customer acquisition cost or lifetime value published for any elder-care startup.
- Practo's revenue split (doctor-side vs consumer-side) was not verified in this research.

## What evidence exists that these do or do not work for older users?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on how these specific products perform for older users is thin. There are no published usability audits of Practo, Apollo 24/7, 1mg or PharmEasy for seniors, and aggregated Play Store review themes could not be retrieved. Indirect evidence points consistently to barriers:
- **Population surveys:** a HelpAge India survey (Jun 2025, 10 cities) found that only 40% of urban elders own a smartphone, 71% use basic phones, 66% find digital tools "too confusing", 51% avoid digital modes for fear of errors, and children or grandchildren do the digital tasks.
- **Indian studies:** perceived risk and technology anxiety are the strongest barriers to elders continuing to use health apps, and usability is the strongest enabler.
- **Product signals:**
  - Practo's 2021 data showed 65% of vernacular consults came from 60+ users.
  - Apollo promotes WhatsApp ordering to bypass a "complex" checkout.
  - The senior-app failure (GenWise) was attributed to adoption and monetisation problems.

### Cited Findings
- **HelpAge India survey "Understanding Inter-generational Dynamics and Perceptions on Ageing"** (released 13 Jun 2025): 5,798 participants (30% aged 60+) in 10 cities (Delhi, Kanpur, Kolkata, Hyderabad, Chennai, Madurai, Bengaluru, Mumbai, Nagpur, Ahmedabad). Findings:
  - 40% of elders own a smartphone; 71% use basic phones.
  - 13% use computers, social media or the internet.
  - 66% find digital tools "too confusing"; 51% avoid digital modes for fear of errors.
  - 54% of children and 52% of grandchildren help elderly family members with digital tasks.
  - Use of health or telemedicine apps was not reported.
  — [Outlook Money 2025-06-14](https://www.outlookmoney.com/news/digital-divide-among-elderly-66-find-digital-tools-too-confusing) [F]
- **Tandon, Ertz and Shashi**, "Continued intention of mHealth care applications among the elderly: An enabler and inhibitor perspective". Survey of 643 older adults (60+) using mHealth apps in North Indian states, analysed with structural equation modelling. Findings:
  - Usability had the strongest effect on engagement, followed by gamification and empathetic cooperation/social interaction.
  - Perceived risk was the strongest inhibitor, followed by sunk cost, technology anxiety and transition cost.
  - Publication year and journal not confirmed; the repository copy cites references up to 2022.
  — [UQAC repository PDF, acc. 2026-11-15](https://constellation.uqac.ca/id/eprint/9494/1/Continued%20intention.pdf) [F]
- **Practo vernacular teleconsult pilot:** 25% of online consults were in vernacular languages; 65% of vernacular consults came from users aged 60+; 67% from non-metro cities. Hindi was most used, then Tamil, Marathi and Kannada — [BioSpectrum 2021-04-07](https://biospectrumindia.com/news/95/18388/practo-teleconsultation-service-now-in-15-indian-languages.html) [F] (older data, 2021)
- **Apollo 24/7:** describes WhatsApp ordering as a way of "avoiding the complex add-to-cart and checkout procedures" — [Apollo 24/7 blog, acc. 2026-11-15](https://www.apollo247.com/blog/article/apollo-247-whatsapp-to-order) [S]
- **PharmEasy:** users without smartphones can order via call centre, but one listing says the phone line applies only in Mumbai and Bangalore — [SilverTalkies, acc. 2026-11-15](https://c1.silvertalkies.com/care/description?id=1042) [S]
- **GenWise shutdown:** sources said it "struggled with user adoption despite modest growth in app usage" and faced "monetisation challenges" — [Inc42 2025-12-09](https://inc42.com/buzz/exclusive-kunal-shah-backed-genwise-shuts-down-operations) [F]
- **Urban Indore community study:** among older non-users of mHealth, reasons were preference for traditional healthcare (42.1%), lack of awareness (31.6%) and digital illiteracy (23.7%) — [IJMEDPH Vol 15 Issue 3 (year not confirmed), acc. 2026-11-15](https://www.ijmedph.org/Uploads/Volume15Issue3/329.%202952.%20IJMEDPH_Vijay%20Khare_1792-1796.pdf) [S]
- **Telemedicine in India, scoping review (2019–2024 studies):** limited smartphone access, unreliable internet and preference for in-person visits were key barriers. Not elder-specific — [Cureus via PMC, 2024-08](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11414145/) [S]
- **Rural southern India:** promoting patient "self-management" via mHealth "may not be as readily translated" to India as proponents assume — [PMC5359738 (2017, older)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5359738/) [S]
- **Rural Karnataka (Mysore/Suttur), 150 older adults:** 50% mobile phone ownership, very few smartphones, digital literacy about 11%, under 10% used the internet to contact health professionals (2022). The link to this exact PMC record is not confirmed — [PMC8938771, acc. 2026-11-15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8938771) [S]
- **Geriatric outpatients (n=121):** most viewed telemedicine positively but felt it could not replace face-to-face consultation (year not confirmed) — [Gerontechnology, acc. 2026-11-15](https://journal.gerontechnology.org/archives/2168-2542-1-SM.pdf) [S]
- **Global scoping review:** usability evaluation methods suited to older adults have "not received attention" (not India-specific) — [PMC9717549 (2022)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9717549/) [S]
- **E-pharmacy reliability (all consumers, COVID era):** a LocalCircles poll of 16,829 consumers found 85% unable to receive medicines via e-commerce/e-pharmacy apps during lockdown (2020, older data) — [LocalCircles, acc. 2026-11-15](https://localcircles.com/a/press/page/ecommerce-medicine-delivery-and-availability-during-covid19) [S]
- **HelpAge's own workaround:** "Community Digital Volunteers" helped 10,000+ elders use government schemes through CSCs. The programme rests on human assistance rather than self-service apps — [HelpAge AR 2024-25, Dec 2025](https://www.helpageindia.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Annual-Report-2024-25.pdf) [F]

### Inferences
- The products that do reach elders at scale rely on a human channel: phone helplines, MHU staff, hospital help desks, care managers, family members operating the app, or WhatsApp/phone ordering. This is consistent with the 66% "too confusing" figure and the 54% child-assistance figure.
- Elders' strong preference for vernacular consults (Practo 2021) points to language as a barrier in English-default interfaces. No 2024-26 update was found.

### Gaps
- No India-specific usability study (task-success or SUS-style) of any named mass-market health app with older users was found.
- Aggregated app-store review themes (e.g. login/OTP problems, font size, cancellation or refund issues) for Practo, Apollo 24/7, 1mg, PharmEasy, Emoha and Khyaal could not be retrieved. Play Store pages did not render for automated fetch, and no third-party review-theme analysis was found. Verbatim reviews are covered by another team member.
- LASI's official national smartphone/internet-use percentages for 60+ were not located in this pass. Other surveys cited here are not directly comparable.
- No published outcome evaluation (adherence, hospitalisation, ER visits) for any elder-care startup subscription.

## Which are urban/English-only/metro-only?

### Takeaway
Almost all private elder-care services are metro- or tier-1-concentrated:
- **Delhi-NCR:** Emoha/Epoch, Samvedna and Antara.
- **South India metros:** Primus.
- **Four metros:** Nightingales at its 2023 acquisition (Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai, Mumbai).
- **Two metros:** Care24 (Mumbai, Delhi).

Anvayaa (38 cities) and Samarth (350 cities) claim wider reach, but these are self-reported. Mass-market apps claim pan-India coverage (Practo: 640+ cities), but phone-ordering fallbacks may be limited (PharmEasy: Mumbai and Bangalore per one listing). The only programmes documented as reaching rural elders are NGO and government ones: HelpAge MHUs in 2,845 villages and Elderline by phone. ### Inferences
- Private elder-care services are geared to urban, middle- and upper-income families, often with children in other cities or abroad. Rural and poor elders depend on NGO MHUs, phone helplines and government schemes.
- None of the elder-care startup apps documented language options. Practo's 2021 vernacular rollout is the only documented multilingual feature among the products reviewed.
